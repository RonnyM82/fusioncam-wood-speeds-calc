Chip load gives you a number. Physical limits decide your feed.

This reference supports a speeds and feeds calculator. It covers plywood, MDF, laminated panel, veneered panel and solid timber on industrial nesting routers. The base calculation is simple. The published data disagrees by a factor of up to 3. A set of physical limits decides the real feed. These limits are spindle torque, vacuum hold-down, corner deceleration and the minimum chip load for MDF.

Layer 1

The core maths

Three equations and one conversion error to avoid. The wood industry works from chip load per tooth. It does not work from surface speed. But surface speed still sets a maximum rpm on large tools.

Chip load per tooth
fz = Vf / (n × Z)
Vf feed mm/min · n rpm · Z flutes. The result is in mm/tooth. Every vendor chart publishes this number.
Feed
Vf = n × Z × fz
Use this form in practice. Select a chip load target and an rpm. The equation gives a feed. A limit then reduces it.
Spindle speed
n = Vf / (Z × fz)
Use this form when the machine limits the feed. It gives the rpm that holds the chip load.
Surface speed
Vc = π × D × n / 1000
SFM = 0.262 × Din × n
This gives m/min from D in mm. It sets a maximum rpm on large diameters. Vendor charts do not show it.
Material removal rate
MRR = ap × ae × Vf
The result is in mm³/min. For a full slot, ae = D. The power calculation uses this result.
Rpm from surface speed
n = (Vc × 1000) / (π × D)
n = SFM × 3.82 / Din
The imperial constant is 3.82. It is not 4. The error increases as the diameter decreases.
Conversion error to avoid

US vendors publish inch/tooth and IPM. European vendors publish mm/tooth and m/min. For chip load, 1 inch/tooth = 25.4 mm/tooth. For feed, the conversion is different: IPM ÷ 39.37 = m/min. Do not divide the feed by 25.4. Cutter Shop states the rule as divide inches per minute by 39.374. If you use the wrong divisor, the feed is wrong by a factor of 1.55.

Feed, rpm and chip load are one relationship

The feed that holds a given chip load on a 2-flute tool. The lines are diagonal. A feed reduction at constant rpm therefore moves the tool below the minimum chip load.

ITA's two datasets don't agree with each other

Work ITA's own numbers backwards and a problem shows up. They give a reference point of 20 m/min at 20,000 rpm on a 12 mm nesting tool, and they separately publish an MDF chip load of 0.10 to 0.20 mm/tooth. Both figures describe the same cut, so they ought to reconcile. They don't.

Do the arithmetic: 20,000 mm/min ÷ 20,000 rpm = 1.0 mm per revolution. Divide that by the flute count and you get 0.50 mm/tooth on two flutes, 0.33 on three, 0.25 on four. You'd need six flutes or more before the result lands anywhere near their published figure.

The key thing to understand is the size of the gap, which runs to a factor of two to five depending on the tool you assume. Three explanations fit. The mm/tooth column could be per revolution rather than per tooth, it could assume a high-flute-count nesting tool, or the two datasets were compiled separately and never checked against each other. Their published material doesn't say which, so I'm not going to guess on your behalf.

Note: this is the strongest argument in the whole reference for making flute count an explicit setting the user chooses, with the source named next to it. Bury the convention in a lookup table and the calculator quietly inherits whichever assumption you happened to code, and you won't find out it was the wrong one until a test cut tells you.

Layer 1

Where the vendors disagree

You'll hit this the moment you start populating tables. Look up a 12.7 mm tool in hardwood across five vendors and the answers span a factor of 2.5 to 3, with the same diameter, the same material and the same nominal conditions behind every one of them. The temptation is to average them and move on. Don't. The spread carries two real signals, the machine class each vendor assumed and the tool geometry each chart was written for, and the geometry signal turned out to be the bigger of the two once the 2017 Onsrud catalogue went under the microscope.

12.7 mm solid carbide in hardwood

Each bar is a published range in mm/tooth, with Onsrud now split out by tool series. The two groups are still there. What's changed is that one vendor appears in both of them.
One vendor spans the whole spread

The key thing to understand is that Onsrud's own catalogue covers nearly the full range by itself. Their 48-000 straight series in hardwood at 12.7 mm sits at 0.152 to 0.203 mm/tooth. Their 60-100C compression chipbreaker, same material, same diameter, same technical data page, sits at 0.533 to 0.584. That's a 3× spread inside one catalogue, and the top of it lands slightly above Vortex. The plain 2-flute spirals (52-200/57-200) and the 3-flute hoggers (60-000) fill in the ladder between them.

The forum lore about conservative Onsrud numbers grew out of their straight and plain-spiral series, because those are the general purpose tools people look up first. Machine class is still real. A CAMheads instructor puts the Vortex-style charts squarely on machines with 10 hp spindles or more, and reports running close to those numbers on a Morbidelli, which is about as direct a confirmation as a forum thread gives you. But line the series up and tool geometry explains at least as much of the spread as the machine does. Nobody on those threads explained the gap, and none of the vendors publish a test method you could use to work out who is right. The catalogue quietly did the explaining for them.

What this means for the calculator

Rule number one: key the chip load envelope by material and tool geometry class first, vendor second. One number per vendor was never going to work, Onsrud alone would need three. Use the chipbreaker and industrial band when the tool genuinely is one of those, keep the straight and plain-spiral values as the conservative floor, and drop back to that floor when the machine is lighter than you'd like, the tool is long, or the part is small enough that hold-down becomes the real problem.

Datapoint · MDF

Two-flute 1/2″ square tool in 1/4″ MDF. Vortex specify 0.025 to 0.027″, i.e: 0.64 to 0.69 mm/tooth. Ted Bruning reported that figure in Woodshop News.

Datapoint · hardwood

Same 1/2″ diameter in hardwood, one catalogue page: Onsrud 48-000 straight at .006 to .008″ against Onsrud 60-100C compression at .021 to .023″. That gap survives with every other variable held constant, so the tool is what's driving it.

What this means for the build

Store every range with its source and geometry class attached, let the user pick a profile, and show the other bands faintly for comparison. Don't store a blended mean. An averaged number is one no vendor will stand behind, so when a test cut disagrees with it you'll have nothing to trace back to.

Layer 1

Depth of cut reduces the chip load

Freud and Onsrud give the same rule. Almost every source agrees on it. Published chip loads assume a depth of cut of one diameter or less.

Chip load multiplier against depth

The depth is a multiple of the tool diameter.
Depth of cutChip loadNote
1 × D100%Use the published value
2 × D≤ 75%Reduce the value by 25% or more
3 × D≤ 50%Reduce the value by 50% or more
  • The standard axial depth is 1×D. Use up to 2×D on larger rigid tools. Vortex permit 2×D for tools of 1/4″ and above, and 1×D below that size.
  • A larger diameter permits much more depth. Use a tool of 12 mm or more for a single-pass nested cut. Do not use a small tool at a large depth.
  • Compression tools add a second rule. A compression tool sets a minimum depth. On some panels the two rules leave no permitted depth.
Limit 01

The minimum chip load for MDF

MDF is abrasive. The cured UF and MUF resin micro-crystals are harder than the wood fibre around them. The tool does not break. Instead the cutting edge becomes round, the binder glazes and the panel edge burns.

Below the minimum, the tool rubs

Chip load per tooth against the condition at the cutting edge.

Plough zone. No chip forms. The edge rubs, the temperature increases and the binder glazes.
Marginal zone. The chip forms intermittently. The edge becomes round quickly.

Cut zone. The chip forms correctly and removes the heat.
Heavy zone. Rigid machines permit this range. Check the spindle power and the hold-down.

The threshold value

Below approximately 0.08 to 0.10 mm/tooth the edge rubs and does not cut. CNCCookbook give the general carbide limit as approximately 0.004″, which is 0.10 mm. Below this value the tool rubs, the tool life decreases and the machine chatters. Minimum-chip-thickness theory agrees. Below approximately 5 to 20% of the cutting edge radius, no chip forms and the tool ploughs.

  • This is a minimum value, not a target value. It is the only limit that increases the feed. It is also the reason a feed reduction often makes the MDF finish worse.
  • Tool life in MDF is 50 to 70% shorter than in solid wood, from vendor and user figures.
  • The rub condition continues once it starts. A round edge needs a thicker chip to cut. A tool that goes below the minimum therefore stays below it.

Measured edge recession, K10 carbide

Per 4,000 linear metres at the same chip load. Source: ZC Tools pre-mill data.

The wear rate in MDF is approximately six times the rate in radiata pine. This value decides whether a coated tool is economic. It also shows where PCD becomes the lower cost per metre, although PCD is outside this scope.

Limit 02

Compression geometry sets a minimum depth

Every other rule reduces the depth of cut. This rule increases it, and it is absolute. The material must fully cover the transition between the up-cut and down-cut zones. If it does not, one face tears out.

Where the transition has to sit

The up-cut zone is at the tip. The down-cut zone is above it. The tool moves chips inward from both faces.
The rule

The up-cut length is usually approximately 1× the cutting diameter. On thin-panel tools it can be as short as 0.4×. Vortex give this instruction: a tool with a 0.5″ up-cut length must cut a minimum of 0.563″ in one pass. This is the up-cut length plus approximately 1.6 mm.

Failure condition

If you cut too shallow with a compression tool, the top laminate chips. An example is 5/8″ material with a tool geometry for 3/4″ material. The tool is sharp and the feed is correct. But the geometry is in the wrong position in the panel.

  • Ramp and plunge at approximately 45° over 2 to 4 inches at approximately one third of the feed. This applies to down-cut and compression tools.
  • Match the up-cut length to the panel thickness. A 0.700″ up-shear tool chips material thinner than 3/4″. Use approximately 0.400″ for 1/2″ material.
  • Check both depth rules together. The depth reduction rule gives a maximum of 1 to 2×D. The compression tool gives a minimum. On a thick panel with a small tool, no single pass satisfies both rules.
Limit 03

Spindle torque below the breakpoint

Industrial router spindles give constant torque below the breakpoint and constant power above it. If you reduce the rpm to hold a chip load target on a large tool, the available power decreases with it. This limit often decides a full-depth nested pass at high feed.

Power and torque against rpm

A 10 kW spindle with a breakpoint at 12,000 rpm and a maximum speed of 24,000 rpm. Change the breakpoint to see the effect of a different spindle.

Below the breakpoint the torque is constant and the power decreases with the rpm. Above the breakpoint the power is constant and the torque decreases as 1/n. If you reduce the rpm to half the rated speed, the available power also falls to approximately half. Torque curves differ between spindles with the same rating. One 3.8 kW HSD-type spindle can be constant from 12,000 to 24,000 rpm. Another reaches its rating only from 18,000 rpm.

Power from torque
PkW = T × n × 2π / 60,000
T is in Nm and n is in rpm. At 12,000 rpm a 10 kW spindle gives 7.96 Nm.
Available power below breakpoint
Pavail = Prated × n / nbp
The decrease is linear. At 6,000 rpm with a 12,000 rpm breakpoint, half the rated power is available.
Torque above breakpoint
T = Prated × 60,000 / (2π × n)
A 10 kW spindle gives 5.3 Nm at 18,000 rpm and 3.98 Nm at 24,000 rpm.
Limit 03b

Cutting power from specific cutting force

Peer-reviewed kc values exist for wood panels and you can use them. They let the calculator convert a proposed cut into a power value in kilowatts. The calculator then compares that value against the spindle curve.

Pcut W = kc N/mm² × MRR mm³/min ÷ 60,000
Pcut W = kc N/mm² × MRR cm³/min ÷ 60
Unit error to avoid

N/mm² and J/mm³ are not numerically equal. 1 J/mm³ = 1000 N/mm². Use the equation above. Do not substitute one unit for the other, or the result is wrong by a factor of 1000. Also, the metal-cutting kc1.1 and mc tables do not apply to wood. Their values are 20 to 100 times too high.

Worked example. The 2.5 kW slot

A 12 mm tool in an 18 mm board. Full-depth slot at 20 m/min.
Why a nesting router needs 10 kW or more

ap 18 × ae 12 × 20,000 mm/min = 4.32 × 10⁶ mm³/min. At kc 35 this gives 2.52 kW at the cutting edge. This value excludes spindle efficiency and the vacuum pump. It also assumes 20 m/min and not 30 m/min. These numbers set the machine class.

Note: kc increases sharply at small chip thickness. This is Kienzle behaviour. Power estimates for light finish passes are therefore too low. The literature gives no accurate Kienzle constants for wood panels. The round-shape papers use a linear Fc = intercept + Ks·h model instead.

Limit 04

Vacuum hold-down limits the feed

A small nested part has a small footprint. The hold-down force changes with the area, but the cutting force does not. Below a given part size, the hold-down sets the feed limit. No change of tool corrects this.

Hold-down against cutting force as parts get smaller

A square part in an 18 mm panel with a 12 mm tool at 0.5 mm/tooth. The hold-down force decreases with the square of the edge length. The cutting force stays constant.

The theoretical hold-down is the full 83 kPa times the area. This is approximately 2.5 times the usable curve on this chart. At the crossover point the part starts to move during the cut. Below the crossover, use an onion skin or tabs. Do not only increase the vacuum.

Theoretical ceiling

At sea level, 1 atm is approximately 14.7 psi or 101 kPa. Practical flow-type tables reach approximately 24″ Hg, which is 12 psi or 83 kPa. Users assume 12 psi as the maximum and reduce it for altitude.

Leakage compounds

The tool opens more of the porous MDF board as the cut continues. The air leakage then increases and the available vacuum decreases. The sheet holds the last parts least well. The offcut frame is also weakest at that point.

Onion skin is the fix

Leave approximately 0.25 mm or 0.010″ and cut through last at a reduced feed. One CNC controller patent does this automatically. It leaves approximately ten thousandths of an inch on parts below a set size and reduces the feed on the final pass.

Limits of this model

Pressure × area gives the theoretical maximum normal force. Friction against the spoilboard resists the sideways movement of the part. That friction force is only a fraction of the normal force. The sources reviewed publish no coefficient for it. The calculator therefore shows the grip factor as a user input. It does not hide an estimated value inside the calculation.

Limit 05

Effective flute count on inserted tooling

This is a secondary requirement for the calculator. It is also the easiest place to set a feed 200 to 300% too high. Runout causes one insert to do most of the cutting. The nominal flute count is therefore incorrect.

What runout does to chip distribution

A head with four inserts. Compare the nominal assumption with the effect of runout.
Use a conservative value

Assume an effective Z of 1 for inserted tooling. Use a higher value only if the head is precision-set with runout held to fractions of a thousandth of an inch. The true value can be 1. If you calculate on the nominal Z, the feed per active insert is too high by the full flute count.

  • Patent literature defines the term effective flutes. It is the number of flutes that must pass to cut one complete profile. On a head with eight helical insert groups that overlap, the effective flute count can be four.
  • The same effect occurs on solid carbide. Collet runout on a six-flute nesting tool loads the flutes unequally and reduces tool life by 20 to 30%. The cause is the same but the effect is smaller.
  • The count for up-cut, down-cut and compression tools is also unclear. Sources disagree on whether to count only the up-cut flutes or all of them. Make this an input. Do not assume a value.

Limit 07

Radial chip thinning

If the radial engagement is less than half the diameter, the true chip is thinner than the programmed chip load. The geometry comes from metal cutting. Wood CAM applies the same equation. But one condition is specific to wood.

The geometry is correct. But Vortex give a practical rule: always use the minimum chip load on the radial depth of cut. If you do not, the tool wears without cutting. Do not use a radial engagement so small that even a compensated feed keeps the chip below the minimum.

Limit 08

Deflection and stick-out

Deflection increases with the cube of the length and decreases with the fourth power of the diameter. No feed value corrects a tool that extends too far from the holder.

Relative deflection against length-to-diameter ratio

The chart normalises the values to 1.0 at a ratio of 3:1. A small increase in length gives a large increase in deflection.

Carbide can cut to a depth of 4 to 10× its diameter if you reduce the feed. The permitted ratio is higher for small tools. Always use the shortest tool that reaches the full depth. Keep the depth per pass at or below 1×D on long tools.

Limit 09

Corner deceleration

The machine decelerates to almost zero at every corner. It then accelerates again after the corner. The chip load therefore decreases to a very low value in the corner. Small parts and small radii burn for this reason.

Distance needed to reach programmed feed

Lmin = v² / a. Three classes of acceleration.
Lmin = v² ÷ a metres, m/s, m/s²
Two examples

At 50 mm/s with an acceleration of 500 mm/s², the machine needs 5 mm to reach the feed. At 20 m/min, which is 333 mm/s, with an acceleration of 2,000 mm/s², the machine needs 55 mm. It needs the same distance again to stop. One forum report describes a machine that needs 3″ to decelerate and 3″ to accelerate. That machine is below the programmed feed for 6″ of every side.

  • The programmed chip load is not the chip load in the corner. On a 120 mm part side at 20 m/min, the machine accelerates or decelerates for most of the side.
  • Use the corner feed option in the CAM system. It reduces the feed by a set percentage over a set distance before and after the corner. The chip load then stays in range.
  • Acceleration is more important than maximum feed for small nested parts. The machine reaches the maximum feed only on long moves.
Limit 10

Laminate edge quality is a geometry problem

The shear angle, the helix, the compression geometry, the chipbreaker, the carbide grade and the edge sharpness control this result. The calculator can give a correct feed for the wrong tool. The panel then still chips.

Field report from WOODWEB

One shop destroyed two $80 compression tools within ten sheets of Wilsonart HPL. The same shop then cut more than thirty sheets correctly with a cheaper straight carbide-tipped tool. The sharp compression edge fractures on hard HPL and on melamine resin. A stronger and less sharp edge does not fracture.

Note this result. On hard laminate, the sharper and more expensive tool was the wrong tool.

Geometry, not feed

Leitz supply positive, neutral and negative shear geometries by application in the Diamaster PRO and PLUS range. Match the shear angle to the face material. Do not match it to a feed target.

Match up-cut to thickness

A 0.700″ up-shear tool chips material thinner than 3/4″. Use approximately 0.400″ up-shear for 1/2″ material. The tool family is correct but the size is wrong.

Abrasive fillers

Melamine and HPL contain aluminium-sulfate and mineral fillers. Use a chipbreaker compression tool of the 3+3 type against this abrasion. At high volume PCD gives the lowest cost, but PCD is outside this scope.

Rule for the calculator advice function

If the user reports laminate edge chips, do not recommend a feed change. Recommend a change of tool geometry. A feed change does not correct this defect and wastes a sheet.

Limit 11

The other limits

These effects are smaller. But each one appears in the literature because it caused a real failure.

Grain direction in solid timber

A cross-grain cut needs a lower feed than a cut along the grain. ITA give hardwood at 8 to 12 m/min along the grain and 5 to 8 m/min across it. Softwoods accept 20 to 30% more feed.

Resin and pitch

Softwood resin builds up on the flute. Clean the tool at regular intervals. A DLC coating reduces this adhesion. This is possibly its largest practical benefit.

Dust extraction removes heat

Extraction removes chips from the cut zone, keeps the flutes clear and removes heat. It is necessary for the cut. It is not only a cleanliness function.

Moisture content

The moisture content of solid timber changes the cut and the finish. Add an input for it in the calculator. No source publishes a correction value, so the user must estimate it.

Climb vs conventional

A nested cut usually uses climb milling for the finish. On the final skin pass or tab pass, the cut direction changes the part stability as much as it changes the finish.

Axial depth rule of thumb

Use 1×D as the standard value. Use up to 2×D on large rigid tools. Use 1×D as the maximum for tools of 1/4″ and smaller.

Build it

What changed in fifteen years

Tooling and machines changed substantially. The chip load advice did not change.

Then and now

The areas that changed, and the area that did not.
Carbide grade

Micrograin and sub-micrograin grades are now standard. They are sharper, more durable and you can regrind them. Infinity state that their optimal-helix micrograin tools last up to 300% longer than standard router tools.

Coatings, with a caveat

DLC and nanocomposite nACo-type coatings are now standard for wood and MDF. Vendors state a tool life increase of 2 to 3 times. Users report that the coating wears off the cutting edge quickly. Part of the benefit is therefore less resin build-up and slower wear on the tool body. Plain TiN gives no useful benefit on wood.

Nesting-specific geometry

Compression tools in 3+3, 2+2 and 3+1 configurations and chipbreaker rough tools are now separate product categories. Three-flute nesting tools for high feed are now standard products.

Spindles and holders

HSK-interface spindles now give higher power. Use shrink-fit HSK63F holders for a nested cut at high feed. ER32 and ER40 collets are adequate to approximately 18 m/min. Above that feed, collet runout loads the flutes unequally and reduces tool life by 20 to 30%.

CAM caught up

Chip thinning compensation in Mastercam Dynamic, Fusion Adaptive and hyperMILL is now standard. Corner feed control is also standard. Neither function now needs custom post processor code.

The data did not move

Chip load advice still comes from catalogues and from custom. Vendors publish start ranges and instruct you to confirm them with a test cut. This has not changed in fifteen years.

Burn marks in corners. Use the CAM corner feed function, or increase the machine acceleration.
Glazed or polished MDF edge. Increase the feed or reduce the rpm to raise the chip load above the minimum.
Parts lift or move. Use an onion skin or tabs. More vacuum does not correct a small footprint.
Torque stall at low rpm. Increase the rpm into the constant-power range, or reduce the depth and the feed.

How to validate the values

Every source states that the published data is a start point. Record the linear metres per tool and correct your own tables against that record. A good 12 mm carbide tool gives approximately 600 to 1,000 linear metres in laminated chipboard before the edge quality falls. Use this figure to check your default values.

Where the data is weak

Read this section. A calculator that shows these numbers to four significant figures suggests an accuracy that the source data does not have.

Chip load data

Vendor charts disagree by 2 to 3 times. Much of the difference follows the tool geometry class. They seldom give the test method. They also serve a marketing function. The values come from custom, not from measurement. Use every table as a start range only, and compare values only within the same geometry class.

Plywood kc

Few sources publish plywood data. Peer-reviewed panel values exist for MDF and particleboard only. Plywood values appear mainly as chart figures. Plywood is also semi-anisotropic, so one kc value is a simplification.

Small-chip power

kc increases at small chip thickness. Power estimates for light finish passes are therefore too low. The literature contains no accurate Kienzle constants for wood panels.

Flute count convention

The sources are unclear for up-cut, down-cut and compression tooling. Make this a user input with a documented default value. The ITA disagreement above shows why.

Inserted tooling

The advice on effective flute count comes from users, not from published data. Use a conservative value unless the head has a precision runout setting.

PCD excluded by scope

PCD is outside this scope. But it controls the cost of high-volume MDF and laminate work. Vendors state a tool life of approximately 50 times that of carbide in MDF. PCD gives the lowest long-term cost per metre.
